At the Kurukshetra eclipse gathering, Draupadi asked each of Krishna's eight chief queens how she had come to marry him. One by one Rukmini, Satyabhama, Jambavati, Kalindi, Mitravinda, Satya, Bhadra and Lakshmana told her story, and the remaining queens spoke last. The whole scene is recorded in the Bhagavata Purana, Canto 10, Chapter 83.

It is one of my favourite passages in the Bhagavata, because it is women talking to women. No sage narrates it for them. Draupadi, who had her own famous svayamvara, sits with the queens of Dwarka and simply asks the question everyone quietly wonders: how did you end up married to him? What follows is eight first-person love stories, each completely different.

## Why were Draupadi and Krishna's queens together at Kurukshetra?

A solar eclipse drew kings, sages, and pilgrims from across the land to Kurukshetra for the sacred bathing. The Yadavas came, and Krishna came with his queens. He met the people of Vrindavan again after long years apart, and he went to see the Pandavas. This setting is the Bhagavata Purana, Canto 10, Chapter 82.

In that gathering the women found each other. Draupadi was the wife of the five Pandavas; the queens were the wives of Krishna. So Draupadi went to them with an honest, curious question, and asked how the Lord had come to marry each of them.

Krishna and Draupadi share one of the few celebrated friendships between a man and a woman in the tradition, so the question comes from warmth, not gossip. She wanted their stories in their own words.

## How did each of Krishna's eight queens marry him?

Each queen married Krishna through a different event, and each told it herself. Here is what every one of the eight said, and where the Bhagavata Purana records it.

| Queen | How she married Krishna, in her own words | Where it is told |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rukmini | Her family meant to give her to Shishupala. She sent Krishna a message, and he carried her away from the wedding "as a lion takes his prey from amidst goats and sheep." | Bhagavata Purana 10.52-54 |
| Satyabhama | Her father Satrajit had falsely accused Krishna of stealing the Syamantaka jewel. Krishna recovered the jewel, cleared his name, and Satrajit gave him both the gem and his daughter. | Bhagavata Purana 10.56 |
| Jambavati | Krishna entered her father Jambavan's cave chasing the same jewel. They fought 27 days until Jambavan recognised Krishna as his Lord Ram, and gave him the jewel and his daughter. | Bhagavata Purana 10.56 |
| Kalindi | She performed long austerities to win Krishna as her husband. He came to her with Arjuna, and agreed to marry her. | Bhagavata Purana 10.58 |
| Mitravinda | Krishna came to her svayamvara, defeated the rival kings, and took her to Dwarka. | Bhagavata Purana 10.58 |
| Satya (Nagnajiti) | Her father set a test: subdue seven fierce bulls. Krishna tamed all seven at once, beat the other suitors, and married her. | Bhagavata Purana 10.58 |
| Bhadra | Her own family invited Krishna, their kinsman, and offered her to him as his bride. | Bhagavata Purana 10.58 |
| Lakshmana | At her svayamvara a fish target was hidden near the ceiling, seen only as a reflection in water below. Others failed; Arjuna only grazed it; Krishna shot it clean through. | Bhagavata Purana 10.58, 10.83 |

Read together, the eight stories make a quiet point. Krishna wins some queens by force of arms, receives some as a gift, and is chosen by others through their own vow. There is no single template. That is the information most retellings flatten into "he had many wives," and it is exactly what the queens themselves refuse to flatten.

## What did Rukmini and Satyabhama say?

Rukmini spoke first, as the chief queen. She described the kings gathered with bows in hand to hand her to Shishupala, and how Krishna cut through them and took her away. In the Vaishnava tradition Rukmini is honoured as the form of Lakshmi, the goddess of fortune, come to be with Krishna.

Satyabhama's account is tied to the Syamantaka jewel. Her uncle Prasena was killed, her father Satrajit blamed Krishna for it, and Krishna set out to clear his own name. When he returned the jewel, a repentant Satrajit offered his daughter along with it.

The two queens sit at the head of the group in most tellings. The Bhagavata does not name a favourite. It shows Krishna giving each queen a full share of himself, each in her own palace, which is the point the story is making about him.

## Who were the 16,100 other queens?

Beyond the eight, the Bhagavata Purana counts 16,100 more queens. They were women held captive by the demon Bhaumasura, also called Narakasura. When Krishna killed him, he freed them (Bhagavata Purana, Canto 10, Chapter 59).

At the gathering, these queens spoke last, as a group rather than one by one. They explained that they were the daughters of kings the demon had defeated, held prisoner until Krishna released and married them. In the Gaudiya and ISKCON tradition their foremost, Rohini, replies on behalf of them all.

Eight principal queens plus 16,100 is where the famous figure of 16,108 wives of Krishna comes from. The round "16,000" you often hear is a shortening of the 16,100.

## Why did Krishna marry 16,000 women?

This is the question people actually ask, and it deserves a straight answer. In the Bhagavata the 16,100 were captives with nowhere to return to. In that society, women taken from their homes were shunned even after rescue.

The traditional Vaishnava reading is that Krishna married them to give them a name, a home, and protection, not out of desire. The scripture frames the marriages as an act of rescue that follows the killing of Bhaumasura.

Modern readers push back on this, and that debate is fair to hold. What the text itself says is narrow and worth keeping straight: the women were freed captives, and marriage was how their honour was restored in that world. Whatever meaning a reader takes from it, that is the reason the Bhagavata gives.

## Was Radha one of Krishna's queens?

No. Radha does not appear among the queens, and Draupadi's question never touches her. The queens are Krishna's Dwarka marriages. Radha belongs to the earlier Vrindavan chapter of his life, as his eternal beloved.

Most traditions do not treat Radha as a wife at all. She is worshipped as Krishna's other half, the [birth of Radharani](/stories/birth-of-radharani) and her love with Krishna sitting on a different plane from the royal marriages of Dwarka. This is why a page about the queens can be complete without her, and why the two subjects should not be confused.
