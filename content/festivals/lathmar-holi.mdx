Once a year, two villages eight kilometres apart argue with each other in colour, and the whole of India comes to watch.

**Lathmar Holi** is the Holi of **Barsana** and **Nandgaon**. Men from Nandgaon, Krishna's village, walk to Barsana, Radha's town, to put colour on the women there. The women meet them with gulal and with long bamboo lathis, and the men take it behind round leather shields, singing. The next day the direction reverses.

## When Lathmar Holi falls

| Day | 2027 | 2028 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Laddu Holi, Barsana | Tuesday 16 March | Saturday 4 March |
| **Lathmar Holi, Barsana** | **Wednesday 17 March** | **Sunday 5 March** |
| Lathmar Holi, Nandgaon | Thursday 18 March | Monday 6 March |

Barsana's day is Phalguna shukla navami, Nandgaon's the day after. Both fall in the bright fortnight, ahead of [Holika Dahan and Rangwali Holi](/festivals/holi) on the full moon.

How far ahead moves. In 2028 there are six days between Barsana's Lathmar and the Holi the rest of India keeps, which is the usual sort of gap. In 2027 there are five, because Phalguna shukla dashami is a **kshaya tithi** that year: it begins and ends between two sunrises and so is skipped from the calendar altogether. Nandgaon still plays on 18 March, as the day after Barsana, but a panchang for that date will show ekadashi rather than dashami. Count from the panchang for the year you are travelling rather than back from Holi.

## What the scripture says

The Holi of Radha and Krishna has a text behind it, and it is not the Bhagavata Purana. It is the **Garga Samhita**, the Sanskrit life of Krishna that the Braj traditions lean on for the Radha lilas.

Its fourth khanda, chapter twelve, describes Krishna arriving in the forest to play colour with Radha and her sakhis, and one line in it is the seed of the whole Barsana and Nandgaon exchange:

> "He has come to Your forest."
>
> <cite>Garga Samhita 4.12.7</cite>

That is the germ of it. He is from somewhere else. He has come here, to her side of Braj, uninvited and grinning, and the women of that forest have something to say about it.

The **lathi is not in the text**. It belongs to Braj itself, to the two villages and the way they have chosen to tell the story on each other for generations. That is worth saying plainly rather than dressing a village custom in a verse it does not have. What Braj added is not less than scripture here; it is scripture lived out by people who believe they are standing in the place it happened.

## What actually happens on the day

The morning belongs to the temple. At Barsana the **Shriji temple**, on Bhanugarh hill above the town, does its worship first, and the day starts there with darshan and with samaj gayan, the antiphonal Braj singing that carries the whole festival.

Then the men arrive.

- The **hurriyare**, the men of Nandgaon, come in groups through the afternoon, in white or in colour, drumming.
- They sing teasing verses at the women of Barsana, the **hurriyarin**, who answer them back in verse. The songs are the point as much as the sticks are.
- The women take up the lathis. The men raise their shields and take it, and keep singing.
- **No man strikes back.** A man who is caught without his shield is not beaten but teased, sometimes dressed in a sari and made to dance.
- Gulal goes up in clouds the whole time, and thandai and sweets go round.

The next day at Nandgaon the men of Barsana walk over and the women of Nandgaon are waiting, and it happens again in reverse.

**Laddu Holi** comes first, at Barsana, the day before. An invitation from Nandgaon is formally accepted, and the answer is given by throwing thousands of laddus over the crowd in the temple courtyard. It is one of the happiest hours in Braj, and it is far less crowded than the day after.

## Why two villages and not one

Because the geography is the story.

**Barsana** rises on a hill and is kept as the town of Vrishabhanu and Kirti, and of their daughter. **Nandgaon** is kept as the village Nanda Baba moved to from Gokul, where Krishna spent his boyhood. Between them lie the groves and the ponds that the Braj lilas are set in.

Every year the two towns replay the distance between them and then close it. That is the whole of it. He comes over, they send him back, and both sides sing about it for two days.

## Going to see it

It is one of the great sights in India and it is genuinely crowded, so plan for that.

**Get there early.** The lanes below the hill fill by midday and the approach is on foot. Reach Barsana in the morning, take darshan at the Shriji temple while it is still possible, and find your place.

**Wear what you will discard,** cover your hair with a cloth, and use natural gulal. Wrap your phone or camera in plastic; a lens will not survive an afternoon of dry colour.

**Stay in Vrindavan or Mathura** rather than in the villages, and go out for the day. Both are about an hour away by road, and Nandgaon and Barsana have little accommodation of their own.

**Leave the play to the people whose play it is.** Watch, get coloured, sing along if you know the tune, and let the singers and the palanquins pass.

## Where this sits in the Braj Holi season

Lathmar Holi is one stretch of a much longer festival. Braj begins Holi at [Vasant Panchami](/festivals/vasant-panchami) in Magha, when the pole goes up and the singing starts, and does not stop for about forty days.

After Barsana and Nandgaon, colour moves through Vrindavan and Mathura, and then to [Holika Dahan and Rangwali Holi](/festivals/holi) on the Phalguna full moon and the day after, which is the Holi the rest of India keeps.

For the town itself and how to visit it through the year, see [Barsana](/temples/barsana). For the whole cycle, see [the festivals of Radha Krishna](/festivals).
