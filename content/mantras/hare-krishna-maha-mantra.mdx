The Hare Krishna maha mantra is a sixteen-word Vaishnava chant made of three names of God: Hare, Krishna, and Rama. It first appears in the Kali-Santarana Upanishad of the Krishna Yajurveda, which calls it the means to cross the age of Kali. In the Gaudiya tradition it reads as a prayer: O Radha, O Krishna, please engage me in your loving service.

> _**Hare Krishna Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna Hare Hare**_
> _**Hare Rama Hare Rama, Rama Rama Hare Hare**_

The whole mantra is only three words repeated. Hare eight times, Krishna and Rama four times each. That is why people can pick it up in a minute and chant it for a lifetime.

## What does the Hare Krishna mantra mean?

Every word is a name of God in the vocative case, the form you use when you call out to someone. So the mantra is not a statement or a request for things. It is calling out to God by name, over and over.

The most common word-by-word reading comes from the Gaudiya Vaishnava tradition, the school of [Radha Krishna](/radha-krishna) devotion that Chaitanya Mahaprabhu began.

| Word | Vocative of | Reading in the Gaudiya tradition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hare | Hara | O energy of the Lord, his internal pleasure potency, understood as Srimati Radha |
| Krishna | Krishna | O all-attractive one |
| Rama | Rama | O reservoir of all pleasure, read as Radha-ramana, the one who delights Radha |

A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, who took the mantra worldwide, translated the whole prayer as "O Lord, O energy of the Lord, please engage me in Your service." Read that way, Hare addresses Radha, and Krishna and Rama address her beloved.

One honest note on Rama. Not everyone reads that name the same way. In the Gaudiya reading above, Rama means Radha-ramana, Krishna himself as the one who pleases Radha. Other Vaishnavas read Rama as Balarama, Krishna's brother, and some read it as Ramachandra, the hero of the Ramayana. The tradition itself allows more than one bona fide conception here, so this is a point where sincere devotees differ rather than a settled fact.

## Where does the Hare Krishna mantra come from?

The mantra's named scriptural source is the Kali-Santarana Upanishad, a short Vaishnava Upanishad attached to the Krishna Yajurveda. In it the sage Narada asks Brahma how to cross the age of Kali, and Brahma answers with these sixteen names, calling them "the destroyer of the evil effects of Kali."

That is the earliest text that gives the mantra as a whole. Individual names of Krishna and Rama, and the practice of chanting the holy name, are far older and run through the Puranas.

The line most often quoted alongside the mantra is not from the Upanishad at all. It is from the Brihan-naradiya Purana, and Chaitanya Mahaprabhu made it famous by quoting it in the Chaitanya Charitamrita (Adi 17.21):

> _harer nama harer nama harer namaiva kevalam_
> _kalau nasty eva nasty eva nasty eva gatir anyatha_

"In this age of Kali there is no other way, no other way, no other way than the holy name of the Lord."

## Why does the Upanishad put the words in a different order?

Here is a detail most pages skip. The order you hear today, Hare Krishna first, is not the order in the Upanishad. The Kali-Santarana Upanishad gives the Rama lines first.

| Source or tradition | Order of the sixteen names | Transparency label |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Kali-Santarana Upanishad | Hare Rama Hare Rama, Rama Rama Hare Hare, Hare Krishna Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna Hare Hare | explicitly in named scripture |
| Gaudiya Vaishnava and ISKCON practice | Hare Krishna Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna Hare Hare, Hare Rama Hare Rama, Rama Rama Hare Hare | taught within a specific tradition |

Both versions are the same sixteen names and thirty-two syllables. Gaudiya Vaishnavas chant Krishna first because for them Krishna is the source and Rama a name within his sweetness. Neither order is wrong. It is a good example of why it helps to know which text a claim comes from.

## Why is it called the maha mantra, the great mantra?

Maha mantra means "great mantra." The Kali-Santarana Upanishad singles these sixteen names out from every other mantra and calls them the special means for this age, the age of quarrel and forgetfulness that Hindu cosmology names Kali Yuga.

The reasoning inside the tradition is simple. A mantra made only of God's names is held to carry God's presence, because the name and the named are considered non-different. So chanting is treated as direct contact, not a reminder of something absent. That is the claim a devotee accepts. Whether the sound also settles your mind is something you can notice for yourself.

## What happens when you chant it?

You repeat the names, and you listen. That is the whole method. You can chant out loud, whisper, or say it silently. You can chant alone on beads or together in song.

The tradition's promise is bhakti, a slow turning of the heart toward Krishna, ripening into love. The everyday effect many people describe, believers and not, is a quieter and more collected mind. I would keep those two apart honestly. The first is the tradition speaking. The second is yours to test.

## How do you chant the Hare Krishna mantra?

There are two classic settings, and most devotees do both.

1. **Japa.** Private chanting on a mala of 108 beads. You hold the mala, say the full mantra once per bead, and one full circuit of 108 is called a round. It keeps count so your attention can rest on the sound.
2. **Kirtan.** Group singing with call and response, often with harmonium, mridanga drum, and karatalas. This is the public, joyful form you may have heard in a temple or on the street.

Initiated members of ISKCON, the movement Prabhupada founded in New York in 1966, vow to chant at least sixteen rounds a day. Sixteen rounds of 108 is 1,728 repetitions of the mantra. For everyone else there is no fixed number. It is better to chant a little with attention than a lot on autopilot.

You do not need permission, a teacher, Sanskrit, or beads to begin. You can start now with the two lines above.

## What are the four regulative principles?

Beyond chanting, ISKCON initiates take four vows, called the regulative principles. They come from Prabhupada's Nectar of Instruction and are meant to steady the mind so the chanting can go deeper.

1. No meat, fish, or eggs.
2. No intoxicants, including tobacco, alcohol, coffee, and tea.
3. No illicit sex.
4. No gambling.

These are vows for initiates, not conditions for chanting. Anyone can chant the mantra without following them.

## How Chaitanya and George Harrison carried it

Two people did the most to spread this mantra. The sixteenth-century Bengali saint Chaitanya Mahaprabhu turned it into a public movement, chanting and dancing it through the towns and villages of Bengal and Odisha. Gaudiya Vaishnavism grew from his example, and the mantra sits at its center.

Four centuries later A.C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada brought it west and founded ISKCON. The Beatle George Harrison then recorded it, produced the Radha Krishna Temple singles, and put "Hare Krishna" into the chorus of his 1970 hit "My Sweet Lord," which is how millions first heard it.
