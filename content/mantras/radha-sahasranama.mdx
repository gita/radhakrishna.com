faq:
  - question: "What is the origin of the Radha Sahasranama?"
    answer: "The versions in circulation trace it to the Narada Pancharatra, a Vaishnava Pancharatra text, where Parvati asks Shiva to reveal the secret he has never told anyone and he answers with the thousand names of Radha. Bhaktivinoda Thakura edited that text and Kusakratha das translated it into English, which is how most people meet it. It is not handed down with a numbered chapter and verse, so the Narada Pancharatra is best held as the hymn's traditional home rather than a line you can look up."
  - question: "What are the benefits of reading the Radha Sahasranama?"
    answer: "The hymn makes its own promise: the closing verses say there is nothing on earth equal to the thousand names of Radha, and that the merit of visiting every holy place comes to a person who simply hears them at home. That is the tradition speaking about itself, and I would read it as such. What you can test yourself is smaller and more honest: an hour of hearing her names is an hour your attention is somewhere kind."
  - question: "Is it 1000 names of Radha or 1008?"
    answer: "Both, and they are the same hymn. Sahasra means a thousand, so the Sanskrit title says one thousand. Devotees usually say 1008 out loud, the same way a japa mala carries 108 beads. Transcriptions also differ from each other, because some lines join two names and some recensions add or drop a few. The list on this page runs to 1,007 lines as sung."
  - question: "How many times should you chant Radha's name?"
    answer: "There is no scriptural minimum you have to hit. Many people chant Radha Radha on a 108 bead mala, once round or several. For the sahasranama itself the usual practice is not counting at all but sitting through the whole hymn once, the way this recording is meant to be heard, start to finish. Attention matters more than the total."

He opens with a spoken address in Hindi explaining why he is taking listeners through the names, then a short kirtan, then the names themselves, then a closing prayer offered at Kishori Ji's feet."

A sahasranama is a hymn of a thousand names. There is one for Vishnu, one for Shiva, one for Lalita, and there is one for Srimati Radharani. This page carries it whole: the spoken introduction, the opening kirtan, all 1,007 name lines as they are sung, and the closing prayer.

The recording is the Radha Sahastra Naam Yatra sung by Shradheya Gaurav Krishan Goswami Ji, who takes listeners through the names the way a pilgrim is taken through Brij, one stop at a time. It opens with the traditional invocation.

> _**Shriman Nitya Nikunj Biharne Namah**_
> _**Shri Bihari Biharini Jo Jayati Shri Swami Haridaso Vijayate Taraoon**_

## The spoken introduction

Shradheya Gaurav Krishan Goswami Ji opens the recording by explaining what he is about to do. This is his address, in Hindi transliteration, exactly as sung.

Sampoorna vishva ne Radha Rani ke naam ka aashraya lekar mere saath Brij ki yatra ki.

Usi kari mein ab main aap sabko Radha Rani ke 1008 namo ki yatra karane jaa raha hoon.

Kyunki woh Krishna ki param aahladini Shakti hain.

Samast Shaktiyaan Shri Radha Rani se prakat huyi hain.

Aur Narad Paanch Ratra mein Maa Parvati ke prashna karne par Bhagwan Shiva ne unko Radha Rani ke 1008 namo ka shravan karaya hain aur kaha hai ki "Radha Naam Sahasrasya Samaanam Naasti Bhutaley"

Ki hey Devi Radha Rani ke sahastra namo ke samaan is dharti par koi mantra koi jap nahi hai Aur jo phal samasta tirthon mein bhraman karne ke baad praapta hota hai woh phal ghar baithey hi Radha Rani ke in namo ka shravan karne se praapta hoga

Jo Radha Rani ke in namo ka shravan karenge unke upar meri Kishori ji ki aur mere Banke Bihari ki vishesh kripa nirantar bani rahegi Aur unke jivan mein sukh, shanti, samriddhi, vaibhav, param anand unko nirantar praapta hoga

To aaiye mere saath milkar hum sab sansar ko bhoolkar Radha Rani ke Sahastra Namo ki yatra par nikal padte hain Aur yehi bhaav rakhte hain ki hum Brij mein, Kunjo mein, Shri Kishori ji ke, Banke Bihari ke Shri charnon mein baithey hue hain

Shri Radhe!

Shri Radhe!

## About this prayer

### Where it comes from

Every published version of the hymn traces it to the Narada Pancharatra, a Vaishnava Pancharatra text. In it Parvati asks Shiva to reveal the most guarded secret in his heart, the thousand names of Radha that he has never told anyone, and he answers. That framing, Shiva speaking to Parvati, is what the singer refers to in his introduction below.

The English translation most people meet came through Bhaktivinoda Thakura's edition and was rendered into English by Kusakratha das. It opens with the same four names this recording opens with: Sri Radha, Radhika, Krishna-vallabha, Krishna-samyuta. That is the recension Gaudiya Vaishnava publishers reproduce, and it is the one sung here.

One thing worth knowing. The Narada Pancharatra is the source the translators and publishers who print this hymn name, consistently. It is not handed down with a numbered chapter and verse the way a Bhagavata passage is. So hold it as the hymn's traditional home rather than as a line you can turn to on a numbered page.

It is also not the only Radha sahasranama. Sanskrit Documents catalogues at least two more: one inside the Rudrayamala, which begins Sri Radha, Radhika, Aradhya, and one inside the Radha Manasa Tantra, which begins Ananta-rupini Radha. Different names, different order, same intent. So if you find a list online that does not match this one line for line, it is probably a different recension rather than an error.

### Why devotees chant it

In Gaudiya Vaishnava theology, Radha is Krishna's hladini shakti, his pleasure potency, and every other energy is understood to come from her. The singer uses exactly that phrase in the introduction: param aahladini shakti. So chanting her names is not treated as praising someone adjacent to God. It is treated as touching the source of the joy in God.

The second reason is the promise the hymn makes about itself. The traditional phalashruti line quoted in this recording is "Radha Naam Sahasrasya Samaanam Naasti Bhutaley", which the singer glosses as: on this earth there is no mantra and no japa equal to the thousand names of Radha, and the merit of travelling to every tirtha comes to a person who simply sits at home and hears them. Published English translations of the hymn carry a matching line, that in this world there is nothing equal to the thousand names of Sri Radha. That is the hymn speaking about itself, the way a phalashruti always does, telling you the fruit of the practice in the language of praise.

The third reason is the plainest one. There is no initiation to get, no Sanskrit to learn first, and no ritual to get right. Hearing counts. That is why this is sung as a yatra, a journey you sit through once from start to finish, rather than a mantra you tick off on a counter. If you want a shorter daily chant instead, the [Hare Krishna maha mantra](/mantras/hare-krishna-maha-mantra) is sixteen words and does the same work.

For who Radha is before you meet her thousand names, start with [Radha Krishna](/radha-krishna) and [who is Radha](/radha-krishna/who-is-radha). If a doubt is what brought you here, the [questions](/questions) section takes those head on.
