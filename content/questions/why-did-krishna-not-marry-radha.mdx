The answer most devotees find and keep is the one Gaudiya Vaishnavism gives: their love was never the kind a wedding could contain, and that is the point of it, not a gap in it. Some texts do describe a wedding. The Garga Samhita sets one at Bhandirvan with Brahma himself officiating, and the Nimbarka tradition holds them eternally wed in the divine realm.

What follows sets each source beside the others, so you can see which tradition is speaking and what it is really saying about their love.

## Did Radha and Krishna ever marry?

Two Sanskrit texts say yes. The Garga Samhita describes Brahma performing their wedding in the Bhandiravan forest, telling the couple that they are already husband and wife and that he will simply make it visible in the way of this world. The Brahma Vaivarta Purana narrates the same wedding.

The Bhagavata Purana tells a different part of the story. It keeps Radha veiled rather than named through the five chapters of the Rasa Lila, and its marriages belong to Krishna's later life in Dwarka. The Vrindavan love and the Dwarka marriages are two different chapters of that life, and Radha belongs entirely to the first.

## What does each text and tradition say about a Radha Krishna marriage?

Radha comes into fuller and fuller view across roughly a thousand years of scripture, from the veiled gopi of the Bhagavata to the central figure of Krishna devotion. Different texts and sampradayas therefore speak of her in different registers, and knowing which one is speaking is the whole point.

So the answer depends on which text is speaking, and every one of them is saying something true about [Radha and Krishna](/radha-krishna). The schools that hold them unmarried and the schools that hold them eternally wed agree on the thing that matters: their union is eternal, and no ceremony either made it or could.

## Why did Krishna marry Rukmini and not Radha?

Because they belong to different stages of his life. Radha belongs to Vrindavan, to Krishna's youth among the cowherds. Rukmini belongs to Dwarka, to Krishna the king. He marries her after leaving Vrindavan behind, in the account at Bhagavata Purana 10.52 to 10.54, where she sends him a letter and he carries her away before an arranged wedding to Shishupala.

Rukmini becomes his chief queen. Tradition counts eight principal queens, the Ashtabharya, and 16,100 more women he rescued and married, for the well-known figure of 16,108 wives. Radha stands outside that count altogether, which the tradition reads as her place rather than her absence.

The Gaudiya reading turns the question around. Krishna's married love in Dwarka is svakiya, love within social sanction. His love with the gopis in Vrindavan is parakiya, love that stands outside it. Chaitanya Charitamrita (Madhya 13.24) treats the parakiya love of Vrindavan as the higher of the two, precisely because it is given freely and asks for nothing in return.

## Is there a deeper reason Krishna and Radha did not marry?

The theological answer is that a marriage would not describe what they are. In Gaudiya Vaishnava thought, Radha is not one more woman Krishna might have wed. She is his hladini shakti, his own pleasure potency, his self turned outward so that love can happen at all. Two who are already one being do not need a ceremony to join them.

Not every tradition frames it that way. The Nimbarka sampradaya holds that Radha and Krishna are eternally wed in the divine realm, so for them the marriage is real, it simply is not an earthly event. Even within the Gaudiya line, Jiva Gosvami accepted a svakiya (married) reading for some devotees, as noted in Chaitanya Charitamrita (Adi 10.85). The point that survives every version is the same: their union is treated as eternal, whatever name is put on it.

That is the honest shape of it. The question assumes a wedding was expected and withheld. The scriptures, read together, suggest the love was never the kind that a wedding could contain.
